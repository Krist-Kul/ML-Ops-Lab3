Computed 2026-10-03 from measurements already committed in Tasks 3 and 4. Machine-readable
record: `reports/lab3-cost.json`, regenerated by `make cost-per-1k`.

**No cloud resource was created or called to produce this file.** `cloud_calls_made: 0`. The
endpoint was deleted at the end of Task 4, and `python scripts/teardown_verify.py --lab 3`

this is being written, which was not true of Task 4. This task is arithmetic over evidence
that already exists, which is the only reason it could be done at all: the Lab 3 budget is
overdrawn.

## The verdict, first

| | THB per 1,000 predictions |
|---|---|
| at 100% utilisation — the ceiling, never achieved | **0.136** |
| at 80% utilisation — a well-packed service | 0.170 |
| **at 25% utilisation — the figure I would plan with** | **0.544** |
| at 5% utilisation — an idle internal service | 2.719 |
| **at 0.60% utilisation — what this lab actually ran at** | **22.49** |
| as actually invoiced, incident included | **41.17** |

**Headline for Drill 3: 0.54 THB per 1,000 predictions at 25% utilisation, 5.09 THB/hour,
10.4 req/s.** That is the planning number. The number this lab *realised* is 22.49, and the
number that was *billed* is 41.17 — 165x and 303x the 0.136 ceiling, 41x and 76x the planning
figure.

The gap is not a pricing error and not a measurement error. It is the whole lesson of the task:
**an hourly-billed endpoint's cost per prediction is set almost entirely by utilisation, and
utilisation is the one term in the formula that is not a property of the machine.** The rate is
a published fact. The throughput is a measurement I repeated. The utilisation is a guess about
the future, and at this volume it moves the answer by over two orders of magnitude.

---

## Method

```
cost per 1,000 = hourly_rate × (1000 / (throughput × utilisation)) / 3600
```

`src/costs.cost_per_1k_predictions`, unchanged. Three inputs, each argued below.

### 1. Hourly rate — 5.09 THB per replica-hour

`n1-standard-2`, 2 vCPU / 7.5 GiB, `asia-southeast1`, 1 replica, on demand. From
`src/costs.SERVING_PRICE_TABLE`, verified 2026-09-30 against the Cloud Billing Catalog API
(service `C7E2-9256-1C43`, Vertex AI) at 33.355 THB/USD:

```
2 vCPU-h × (0.045000 + 0.005870 mgmt) + 7.5 GiB-h × (0.006000 + 0.000783 mgmt)
  = 0.152613 USD/h = 5.09 THB/h
```

Two things about this rate that are easy to get wrong and would both understate the bill:

- **It is a prediction SKU, not a Compute Engine SKU.** The N1 prediction core is 0.045000
  USD/vCPU-h against 0.038999 for the training core the Lab 2 table is built on — serving the
  same machine type costs ~13% more per vCPU-hour than training on it. `serving_hourly_rate()`
  is deliberately a separate function from `hourly_rate()` so that passing a serving instance
  to the training table cannot silently return the cheaper number.
- **It is charged per replica-hour whether or not anything calls the endpoint.** This is the
  entire difference between Lab 2's cost model and Lab 3's. A training job that finishes stops
  costing money. An endpoint does not, and that is what the 194.6 THB incident in Task 4 was.

### 2. Throughput — 10.4 req/s

Task 3 produced three throughput numbers and they differ by 5x. Picking the wrong one is the
easiest way to publish a cost figure that is off by 5x in the flattering direction:

| candidate | req/s | why not / why |
|---|---|---|
| local container, saturated | 51.28 | wrong machine. Emulated amd64 on an arm64 laptop, and **it bills nothing** |
| deployed endpoint, client side from Bangkok | 10.27 | includes my home network and ~95 ms of front door; measures my ISP as much as the service |
| **deployed endpoint, container side** | **10.4** | **the machine that bills, timed by itself** |

The third is the service's own `latency_ms` structured log line, read back out of Cloud Logging
for the same requests the client measured. It is the saturated rate — the ceiling at 10 and 50
concurrent callers, where adding callers stops adding throughput:

```
n1-standard-2 container side:   1 VU  →  5.3 req/s    (not yet saturated)
                               10 VU  → 10.4 req/s    ← the ceiling
                               50 VU  →  9.9 req/s    (queueing, no errors)
```

Using the saturated rate rather than the single-caller rate is the generous choice: it assumes
the endpoint is being driven hard enough to reach its ceiling. The cost per prediction at 1 VU
would be about twice as bad.

The 25% row is the one I would plan with for a real internal service with business-hours
traffic. I am not claiming this lab achieved it.

## Predictions are not requests, and on this service the gap is 100x

Everything above prices **one scored row per HTTP request** — the `/predict` route. But
`/predict/batch` takes up to 100 rows, and Task 3 measured the fact that makes that nearly
free: `predict_proba` costs 25.029 ms for 1 row and 25.053 ms for 100. The scoring cost is
flat in rows across the whole range the schema allows. A 100-row call costs what a 1-row call
costs.

So on the same hardware at the same hourly rate:

| utilisation | `/predict`, 1 row/call | `/predict/batch`, 100 rows/call | ratio |
|---|---|---|---|
| 100% | 0.1360 | 0.00117 | 116x |
| 80% | 0.1699 | 0.00147 | 116x |
| 25% | 0.5438 | 0.00469 | 116x |
| 5% | 2.7190 | 0.0234 | 116x |
| 0.60% realised | 22.49 | 0.194 | 116x |

**A cost per 1,000 predictions that does not say which route it means is useless.** Two
engineers can report figures 116x apart from the same endpoint, the same price list and the
same load test, and both be arithmetically correct.

One honesty mark on that column: the batch row rate, 1,206 rows/s, is **projected, not
measured**. Task 3 load-tested the batch route only on the local container, where it hit
3,537 rows/s and a 97x row-throughput gain. The projection is 100 rows divided by the
endpoint's measured single-call p50 of 82.9 ms, which holds only because the flat-in-rows
result was measured on both sides. The endpoint batch route was never put under k6, and with
the endpoint gone it cannot be now. Treat 0.00117 as the right order of magnitude, not a
measurement.

plus a bug. Quoting only the first hides the incident; quoting only the second blames the unit
economics for a missing timeout.

## At what request volume is batch inference cheaper than keeping this endpoint warm?

**Below about 895,000 predictions a day — a sustained 10.4 req/s, which is this endpoint's
saturated ceiling — a once-daily batch job on the same machine type is cheaper, because it
bills for the minutes it runs instead of 24 hours of waiting. If the batch job uses the 100-row
route, that threshold rises to roughly 104 million predictions a day.**

This lab served 5,290 predictions in total. The whole lab's volume, run as a single batch job,
would have cost **1.14 THB** against the 217.78 THB that was spent — and 0.42 THB of that 1.14
is assumed start-up, not scoring.

The derivation, since the shape of the answer is more useful than the number:

```
warm endpoint      5.09 × 24                      = 122.16 THB/day, flat, any volume
batch job          5.09 × (startup + N/R)/3600    = grows with N

break-even N       (122.16 − startup cost) / 5.09 × 3600 × R
```

With `R = 10.4 rows/s`, `N = 895,440/day`. With `R = 1,206 rows/s`, `N = 103,860,072/day`.

The 895,440 figure is **exactly the endpoint's own saturated throughput sustained around the
clock**, and that is not a coincidence — it is the general result. Both options bill the same
rate for the same machine; the endpoint just also pays for its idle time. So an always-on
endpoint only breaks even against batch at 100% utilisation, and anything less is the price of
latency. There is no volume at which the endpoint is cheaper *and* under its ceiling.

Start-up cost is assumed, not measured: 300 s of billed node time (0.42 THB), bounded by the
42.4 s cold start measured on this machine in Task 3 and rounded up generously for job
orchestration. It barely matters — it moves the break-even by 0.35%, because the comparison is
dominated by 24 hours of idle.

`src.costs.batch_breakeven_rps` returns 0.0014 req/s for the same inputs. That function is not
wrong, it answers a different question: it models batch as a *fixed* daily cost regardless of
volume, so it reports the rate below which a fixed-price job wins. Task 5 asks for the
volume-dependent form, which is the one above.

## So what should this service have been?

The cost table makes the recommendation for me, and it is not "tune the endpoint":

| option | THB/h idle | THB/1,000 at 25% | note |
|---|---|---|---|
| `n1-standard-2` endpoint (what was built) | 5.09 | 0.544 | misses the 50 ms p95 target at one caller |
| `e2-standard-2` endpoint | 3.58 | 0.383 | 29.7% cheaper, same 2 vCPU shape, untested here |
| `n1-standard-4` endpoint | 10.18 | 0.639 | +100% cost for +70% throughput — 17.5% *worse* per prediction |
| scale-to-zero service | ~0 idle | ~0 at this volume | Vertex endpoints require `min_replicas ≥ 1`; this needs a different product |
| once-daily batch job | 0 | 0.216 single-row at this lab's 5,290-row volume (start-up dominated), 0.0012 on the 100-row route at scale | no latency guarantee at all |

`n1-standard-4` is the trap. It is the obvious lever, it genuinely improves latency by 42.6%
and cold start by 47.8%, and it makes every prediction 17.5% more expensive. Bigger machines
buy latency. They do not buy efficiency, because the price scales with the vCPU count and the
throughput does not.

The real optimisation at 0.60% utilisation is not on this table's top three rows: **Vertex AI
endpoints cannot scale to zero**, and a service with this traffic profile belongs on something
that can. That is a product choice made before the first deploy, not a tuning knob afterwards,
which is why it costs 217 THB to discover it in week three rather than 0 THB to reason about it
in week one.

Two free changes Task 3 identified and I did not apply mid-measurement, which would move the
throughput term rather than the rate term: `n_jobs=1` at load time (4.1x on the dominant
`predict_proba` cost — `n_jobs=-1` spends ~11 ms per call on joblib dispatch and buys nothing
at inference) and `uvicorn --workers 2` (~2x throughput, at the cost of doubling cold start
toward the 60 s probe cliff). Either would cut the cost per prediction without touching the
hourly rate. Both are unmeasured on the endpoint and are therefore projections.

## What this figure does not include

Serving compute only. Not in the 0.544:

- **Artifact Registry storage** for the serving image, and the GCS objects holding four model
  versions. Small, continuous, and billed after the endpoint is gone.
- **Egress** — the responses to 5,290 predictions, trivial here and not trivial for a batch
  route returning 100 rows per call.
- **Cloud Logging ingest**, which Task 3 leaned on hard: one structured line per request, read
  back for 2,033 requests. Logging a `latency_ms` per prediction is how the container-side
  number exists at all, and at a million predictions a day it is a line item.
- **Training and tuning**, in Lab 2's tables under the training SKUs, which are cheaper per
  vCPU-hour than these.

Lab 5 reconciles all of it against actual billing by tag. The number above is a unit cost for
one component, computed from a published rate and a measurement I repeated — not a forecast of
an invoice.
